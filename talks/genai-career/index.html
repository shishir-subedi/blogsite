Part 1 · What is an LLM?

ChatGPT is a very good next-word predictor

It reads the words so far, scores every possible next word, picks one, adds it, and repeats. It does this so well that we often can't tell AI writing from human writing.

↺ Add the word to the text, then predict again, until the answer is finished

It generates. It does not check. That's why it can sound confident and still be wrong.

  • Ask the room: "The capital of Nepal is ___?" Everyone says Kathmandu. "You just did what an LLM does."
  • Explain: a sentence of 20 words = this loop running 20 times.
  • Old ChatGPT (early 2023) only did this, answering from what it learned in training.

      Calculus
      
        
        
       
      
        
      Models learn by gradient descent: step downhill along the slope. It can get stuck in a local minimum.
      
        
      
          
          
          
        
      
      
      
    
      
    
      You already have these in your syllabus. They are what's inside ChatGPT.
      
  
      
  
        
    
      • You don't need math to use AI. You need it to build AI or go into research.
        • 
    
      • The ball is gradient descent: each step moves opposite to the slope. On entering the slide it starts on the right and gets stuck in the local minimum.
        • 
    
      • Analogy: walking down a hill in thick fog. You only see your feet. You reach a small valley and think you're at the bottom.
        • 
    
      • Click "Start left": it reaches the global minimum. Same method, different starting point, very different result.
        • 
    
      • Real training uses tricks to avoid getting stuck: momentum, random starting weights, and noise from mini-batches.
        • 
    
      • How to read the curve: left to right = different settings of the model's weights (here 1 weight, in ChatGPT billions). Height = error, how wrong the model is. Training = finding the lowest point.
        • 
    
      • Global minimum = the best possible settings, lowest error anywhere. Local minimum = settings where every small change makes things worse, so the model stops, but a much better setting exists somewhere else.
        • 
    
      • Example, spam filter: stuck in a local minimum, it learns "emails with the word FREE are spam". It catches some spam and stops improving, because small tweaks don't help. At a better minimum it learns many signals together: strange links, unknown sender, urgent tone.
        • 
    
      • Example, Pathao or Google Maps: at every junction you take the road that looks shortest right now, and end up stuck in Kalanki traffic. Every local choice looked good, but the best route overall was a different road. Choosing the best-looking small step every time can trap you.
        • 
    
      • Example, career (ties back to your story): a comfortable path where every small change looks worse is a local minimum. Sometimes you need a big jump to reach a better valley, like I did moving from ECE to computer science.
        • 
    
      • How the fixes map: momentum = a ball rolling with speed carries over small bumps. Random starts = try from many places and keep the best. Learning rate = step size: too big and it jumps around, too small and it takes forever.
        • 
    
      • Honest note if someone asks: in very large networks, most local minima turn out almost as good as the global one. The bigger practical problems are flat areas where the slope is nearly zero and learning slows down. Nobody finds the true global minimum for ChatGPT. The goal is an error low enough to work well.
        • 
  
      

      



      
  
      
    
      Calculus in action · the base of deep learning
      
    
      Backpropagation: how a neural network learns
      
    
      Guess, measure the error, use derivatives to see which way each weight should move, then adjust. Repeat millions of times.
      
    
      
      
      
        
        
      
          Ready
        
      
        
      
          
          
          
        
      
        
      
          
      
            
      RoundwPrediction ŷError L
      
            
      No rounds yet
      
          
      
        
      
      
      
      
      
        
      Task: learn the price of one plate of momo. 2 plates cost Rs. 300. The model starts with a bad guess, w = 100.
      
        
      
        
      A negative derivative means "increase w to reduce the error". After a few rounds w reaches about 150, the real price per plate. Nobody told it 150. It learned from data.
      
        
      
          
      forward →input→layer 1→layer 2→… layer 96→error
      
          
      ← backward: chain rule through every layer, for billions of weights
      
        
      
        
      PyTorch and TensorFlow calculate these derivatives automatically (autograd). Knowing what they do is what lets you debug training.
      
      
      
    
      
  
      
  
        
    
      • Click Forward pass: the model predicts 2 × 100 = 200. Real answer 300. Error = 10,000.
        • 
    
      • Click Backward pass: this is the chain rule from your calculus class. dL/dw = dL/dŷ × dŷ/dw. The red arrows show the error flowing back to the weight.
        • 
    
      • Click Update weight: w moves opposite to the derivative, 100 → 120. Learning rate 0.05 controls the step size.
        • 
    
      • Then click Train 10 rounds and watch the error column drop to nearly zero while w approaches 150.
        • 
    
      • Big picture: ChatGPT trains the same way, with the same chain rule applied backwards through many layers and billions of weights. This is why calculus matters for deep learning.
        • 
    
      • Why square the error? 1. Signs don't cancel. Predicting 200 for a Rs. 300 order is off by −100. Predicting 400 is off by +100. Both are equally wrong. Without squaring, averaging −100 and +100 gives 0, which looks perfect. Squared, both are 10,000.
        • 
    
      • 2. Big mistakes cost much more. Off by 10 → error 100. Off by 100 → error 10,000. The model works hardest on fixing its worst mistakes.
        • 
    
      • 3. Easy, smooth derivative. The derivative of (ŷ − y)² is 2(ŷ − y): the slope is proportional to how wrong you are. Far from the answer, big steps. Close to it, small careful steps, automatically. That's why w moved 100 → 120 → 132 → … and slowed down near 150.
        • 
    
      • Why not just |ŷ − y| (absolute error)? It has a sharp corner at zero, where the derivative jumps from −1 to +1. The slope stays the same size however close you are, so it tends to overshoot. Squared error is a smooth bowl.
        • 
    
      • Statistics link: minimising squared error finds the average (mean). It's the same idea as least squares in your statistics course.
        • 
    
      • If someone asks "Does ChatGPT use squared error?" No. Squared error is for predicting numbers: prices, temperatures, marks. For choosing the next word, models use cross-entropy loss, which punishes giving a low probability to the correct word. The backprop and chain rule part is exactly the same.
        • 
    
      • Downside to mention if asked: because big errors are squared, one crazy data point (an outlier, like a typo of Rs. 30,000 instead of 300) can pull the whole model towards it.

      • Analogy: Route 53 is the phone book, the load balancer is the receptionist sending you to a free counter, the servers are the staff, the database is the locked record room, S3 is the warehouse for photos.
        • 
    
      • Security point: the database sits in a private subnet with no internet access. Only the backend can talk to it. That's the same "least access" idea as the agent safety slide.
        • 
    
      • Scaling point: during a Dashain sale, add more servers behind the load balancer. Users never notice.
        • 
    
      • If someone asks: in production many teams also put the frontend and backend servers in private subnets and keep only the load balancer public, and put CloudFront (a CDN) in front of S3 for faster images.
